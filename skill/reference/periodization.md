# Periodization & Progressive Overload

## Method

Friel's annual structure sets the season: periods, weekly hours, recovery weeks and when to test. The 80/20 method sets intensity: zones (`zones.md`), sessions (`workouts.md`), and about 80% of time in Zones 1–2. Strength, testing and recovery monitoring follow `strength.md`, `zones.md` and `load-management.md`.

## Annual Plan (Friel)

Plan each A race backwards from race day:

| Period     | Length         | Abilities                                 | Strength phase |
| ---------- | -------------- | ----------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Prep       | 2–4 weeks      | AE, SS                                    | AA             |
| Base 1–3   | 3–4 weeks each | AE, SS, MF; ME from Base 2; AnE in Base 3 | MT, then MS    |
| Build 1–2  | 3–4 weeks each | ME, AnE, SP, race-specific                | SM             |
| Peak       | 1–2 weeks      | ME, race-specific                         | SM, light      |
| Race       | 1 week         | race                                      | none           |
| Transition | 1–4 weeks      | none, unstructured                        | AA             |

Abilities: AE aerobic endurance, SS speed skills, MF muscular force, ME muscular endurance, AnE anaerobic endurance, SP sprint power. In 80/20 terms, AE and SS are easy (Zones 1–2, plus strides and drills), ME is threshold (Zone 3), and MF, AnE and SP are hard (Zones 4–5, force and hill reps). Strength phases: AA anatomical adaptation, MT max transition, MS max strength, SM strength maintenance (`strength.md`). Mapping to 80/20 zones: Claude's judgement.

- Each 3–4-week block ends with a recovery week, and the recovery week carries the tests (`zones.md`). Its abilities are AE, SS and testing.
- With two A races in a season, run a short Transition after the first, then rebuild from Base 3 rather than from Prep.
- Base periods are longer than athletes want. Most adaptations take months (see "Adaptation Timelines Summary" below).

## Intensity Distribution (80/20)

- **Most training sits below the first threshold**, for events from minutes to many hours (Seiler; research). The long ride and long run are the core of the week, not filler.
- **The amateur trap:** at 6–10 hours a week, an athlete can go medium-hard every session, and stagnates. Easy days stay easy so quality days can be quality (Seiler; research on elite distribution, coach's experience for amateurs).
- **Precision matters more as volume rises.** Up to about 10 hours a week almost anything works for a while. Toward 15–20 hours, sloppy intensity control stops progress (Bu; coach's experience).
- **Hard is sustainable, not maximal.** Quality sessions end with one more rep in the tank. An athlete who turns every interval session into a toughness test stagnates (Seiler; coach's experience).
- **Health first.** An athlete fighting infection, inflammation or low energy diverts resources from adaptation, so the training is wasted (Seiler; research).
- **Other models exist.** Bakken's Norwegian sub-threshold method makes Zone X reps the main quality work, with easy running below about 70% of max HR (coach's experience). A 2024 meta-analysis found no time-trial advantage for polarized training over other distributions (Silva Oliveira 2024; research), so neither model wins on evidence. Pick one per plan rather than mixing them. This skill builds plans on 80/20; switching models is a season-review decision.
- **Train to train.** General physical preparation and fixing weaknesses happen early in the season; the last 6–8 weeks before an A race are for the sport (Galpin, Edgley; coach's experience).

## Macrocycle Structure

Divide the training period into phases:

### 1. Base Phase (40-50% of available time)

- Build aerobic capacity
- Increase volume gradually (max 10% per week)
- Mostly Zone 2 (conversational pace)
- Focus on technique, especially in swimming

### 2. Build Phase (30-40% of available time)

- Introduce intensity (tempo, threshold work)
- Sport-specific workouts
- Race-simulation sessions (brick workouts for tri)
- Peak volume weeks occur here

### 3. Peak/Race-Specific Phase (10-15% of available time)

- Reduce volume, maintain intensity
- Race-pace work
- Course-specific preparation (hills, open water)

### 4. Taper (1-3 weeks depending on event)

- Significant volume reduction (40-60%)
- Maintain some intensity
- Focus on rest, nutrition, mental prep
- Details in "Taper" below

## Race-Specific Phase (final 12 weeks)

- **Train the race demand.** Start from what the race requires (distance, terrain, heat, technical sections) and make the key weekly sessions rehearse it. Conditions count: training only in air-conditioning or before dawn prepares the athlete for the cool (Bu; coach's experience).
- **Race-pace dose:** for half and full Ironman, 80/20 adds about 4–6 race-pace swims, rides or runs (bricks count) of increasing duration over the final 12 weeks, with about 80% of time still easy (Fitzgerald; research summary). Race-pace work at these distances sits in or near Zone X, so it stays a small dose. Fitzgerald caps the longest race-pace sessions for Ironman at 1.2 mi swim, 70 mi bike, 16 mi run; he gives no 70.3 numbers.
- **Race pace is a number, not a zone.** Set it from the latest tests and use it in the rehearsals.
- **Know it before race day.** By the end of Build the athlete should already have held race pace in training, so race day builds on confidence (Bu; coach's experience). To measure capacity, hold the target power or pace for the target duration and see where it breaks: HR drifting up more than about 10 bpm, or the target no longer holdable (Bu; Claude's judgement for the threshold).
- **Cover the run.** Long-course swim and bike legs are usually covered by normal training; the run leg is not (Bu; coach's experience). Build the longest run toward the expected race-run time, as far as injury history and consistency allow, plus regular bricks. A missed week costs more than a long run gains.
- **Long-run variations** for durability: long easy runs that finish with Zone 3 (RFF, RLFF) or carry short surges (RLSP), rather than threshold sets moved into long runs (Bakken, That Triathlon Show hosts; coach's experience).

## Taper

The model: fitness is stable and fatigue is transient, so cut fatigue and keep fitness. Taper evidence is large and consistent (Galpin's summary of the literature; research):

- **Length:** about 7–21 days for endurance races.
- **Volume:** down 40–60%. Never raise volume during a taper.
- **Intensity:** keep it. If intensity is kept, volume must drop at least 25% to give any benefit.
- **Frequency:** keep sessions within about 20% of normal, or the athlete feels sluggish and loses feel (Galpin; coach's experience).
- **Shape:** step, linear or exponential; none is clearly better.
- **Gain:** about 3–6% in time-trial performance. Athletes coming off the biggest overload gain most; a short or light block needs little taper.
- **Individual:** failed tapers are reported as too long and too short about equally. Size the taper from the athlete's own evidence: the day in each recovery week that "legs fresh again?" turns yes (`load-management.md`).

A deload is a short planned or reactive back-off every 3–4 weeks inside training; a taper is the longer reduction before a target race. A plan has both. Last heavy strength session about 10 days before the race (`strength.md`).

## Changing the Plan

- **One change at a time.** Raise the quality of what the athlete already does in small steps. Big overhauls get nodded at and quietly not done (Bu; coach's experience).
- **Never cram missed sessions.** Moving a key session within the week is fine if hard days stay separated; otherwise drop it. One session makes no visible difference; the season is the sum of a few hundred sessions done consistently (Seiler; coach's experience).
- **Swap freely within a family.** Replacing a library workout with a similar one for logistics keeps the week intact (Seiler; coach's experience).
- **Fixed weekly template.** The same session types on the same days let each week learn from the last execution of the same session. A weekly menu of key sessions tends to push the hard ones to the end of the week (Bu; coach's experience).
- **Every session has a purpose** the athlete can say in one sentence (Bu, Edgley; coach's experience).
- **Enjoyment is part of the method.** Keep sessions the athlete likes and understands; adherence beats a perfect plan (Seiler, Bu; coach's experience). A session type the athlete declines is a `declined` row in `Decisions`, not a sticking point.
- **Elite details don't transfer.** What reaches a book or podcast is a small slice of what made an elite successful (Bu; coach's experience).
- **Protect sleep** over an extra session. Work stress, exams and short nights add to the same stress total as training (Seiler; research).
- **After an illness,** restart at lower volume once the athlete is well, and test only after resting HR and HRV are back to baseline.

## Microcycle Structure (Weekly)

### Principles (more important than specific days)

1. **Separate hard days** - 48hr between quality sessions in the same sport
2. **No same sport back-to-back** - Spread swim/bike/run across the week (no swim Mon + swim Tue)
3. **Respect long session recovery** - Easy day after long rides/runs
4. **Consistent weekly structure** - Same sports on same days each week helps athletes plan
5. **Include doubles strategically** - AM/PM splits for time efficiency or brick practice
6. **One quality session per sport** in Base. Alternate week to week between threshold (cruise intervals) and force or hills (Claude's judgement).
7. **Hills and force reps are the week's hard session**, replacing that sport's threshold session, not added to it (Bakken; coach's experience).
8. **Recovery window:** at least one easy day after threshold work; 1–3 after harder work, ideally two after the hardest run session (Bakken; coach's experience).
9. **Hard intervals mostly on the bike.** Keep Zone 4–5 running small: the run comes last in a race, so running strong under fatigue matters more, and fast running carries more injury risk (That Triathlon Show hosts; coach's experience).
10. **Strides all year,** once or twice a week: 4–6 × 20 s at about 90% of top speed, in an easy run or the warm-up of a quality run (Norwegian coaches study; research, descriptive).
11. **Judge an easy session by its effect on the next key session.** An easy ride done a bit hard can blunt the quality session one or two days later (Bu; coach's experience).
12. **Swim three times a week** for an athlete who wants to improve, growing toward about 3 km each: one longer aerobic swim, one threshold, one with short hard reps. Adult-onset swimmers take years to reach a good level (Ford; coach's experience). Technique work: `swim-technique.md`.
13. **Bricks and strength** are not in the 80/20 library; schedule them separately.

### Avoid These Patterns

- ❌ Same sport on consecutive days (swim Mon + swim Tue, run Thu + run Fri)
- ❌ Quality run day after long ride (insufficient recovery)
- ❌ Two threshold sessions in same sport within 48hr
- ❌ Long sessions back-to-back without one being easy

## Progressive Overload

**The 10% Rule**: Increase weekly volume by no more than 10% per week—**for athletes building new fitness**.

### Adjust Progression Based on Athletic Foundation

| Foundation Level             | Progression Rate        | Rationale                            |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| True beginner                | 5-10% per week          | Everything is new; high injury risk  |
| Some experience (1-2 years)  | 10% per week            | Standard progression                 |
| Strong foundation, returning | 10-15% per week         | Body remembers; rebuilding is faster |
| Recent high fitness          | Maintain or small build | Already fit; focus on specificity    |

**Example**: An Ironman finisher who took 3 months off can progress faster than a beginner. Their muscles, tendons, and cardiovascular system have adapted before. Where a beginner might take 20 weeks to reach 10-hour training weeks, someone rebuilding might get there in 10-12 weeks.

**However**: Always validate with the athlete. Time off due to injury requires more caution than time off due to life circumstances.

### 3:1 or 4:1 Loading

- 3 weeks building load, 1 week recovery (reduce volume 30-40%)
- Or 2 weeks building, 1 week recovery for older/injury-prone athletes
- Athletes rebuilding can often handle 3:1; beginners may need 2:1

### Long Session Progression

For a target marathon (42km), build the long run over 12-16 weeks:

```
Week 1:  16km
Week 2:  18km
Week 3:  20km
Week 4:  14km (recovery)
Week 5:  20km
Week 6:  22km
Week 7:  24km
Week 8:  16km (recovery)
...continue to 32-35km peak
```

Never increase the long session by more than 2-3km (running) or 15-20km (cycling) per week.

## Event-Specific Training Requirements

### Full Ironman (140.6)

**Minimum training time**: 12-15 hours/week at peak
**Minimum build time**: 24-30 weeks from solid base

**Weekly structure at peak**:

- Swim: 3x per week, 8-12km total
- Bike: 3x per week, 250-350km total, one ride of 150-180km
- Run: 3-4x per week, 50-70km total, one run of 28-32km

**Key workouts**:

- 5-6 hour bike followed by 30-60min run (brick)
- Long swim of 3500-4000m continuous
- Back-to-back long sessions on weekends

### 70.3 / Half Ironman

**Minimum training time**: 8-12 hours/week at peak
**Minimum build time**: 16-20 weeks from solid base

**Weekly structure at peak**:

- Swim: 2-3x per week, 6-8km total
- Bike: 2-3x per week, 150-200km total, one ride of 80-100km
- Run: 3x per week, 35-50km total, one run of 18-22km

### Marathon

**Minimum training time**: 6-10 hours/week at peak
**Minimum build time**: 16-20 weeks

**Weekly structure at peak**:

- 5-6 running days
- 60-90km per week for experienced runners
- One long run (28-35km)
- One quality session (tempo or intervals)
- One medium-long run (18-22km)
- Remaining runs easy

## Physiological Adaptations: The "Why"

### Why Easy Training Works (Zone 2)

| Adaptation                      | Timeline   | Mechanism                                    |
| ------------------------------- | ---------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Increased mitochondrial density | 4-8 weeks  | More cellular powerhouses for aerobic energy |
| Capillarization                 | 6-12 weeks | More blood vessels deliver oxygen to muscles |
| Improved fat oxidation          | 4-6 weeks  | Shifts fuel use from glycogen to fat         |
| Increased stroke volume         | 8-12 weeks | Heart pumps more blood per beat              |
| Type I fiber development        | Ongoing    | Slow-twitch fibers become more efficient     |

Zone 2 is the foundation—never sacrifice it for more intensity.

### Why Threshold Training Works (Zone 4)

| Adaptation                | Timeline  | Mechanism                                           |
| ------------------------- | --------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Lactate clearance         | 2-4 weeks | Muscles better at processing lactate                |
| Raised lactate threshold  | 4-8 weeks | Sustain higher intensity before lactate accumulates |
| Improved muscle buffering | 3-6 weeks | Tolerate higher lactate levels                      |
| Mental toughness          | Immediate | Learn to sustain "comfortably hard"                 |

### Why VO2max Intervals Work (Zone 5b)

| Adaptation                | Timeline   | Mechanism                          |
| ------------------------- | ---------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Increased VO2max          | 4-8 weeks  | Maximum oxygen uptake improves     |
| Cardiac output            | 6-12 weeks | Heart pumps more blood per minute  |
| Neuromuscular recruitment | 2-4 weeks  | Better coordination at high speeds |

Use sparingly—1x/week max, requires 48-72hr recovery.

### Adaptation Timelines Summary

| System                       | Time to See Changes | Time to Optimize |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------- |
| Neuromuscular (coordination) | 1-2 weeks           | 4-6 weeks        |
| Lactate clearance            | 2-4 weeks           | 6-8 weeks        |
| Aerobic enzymes              | 3-4 weeks           | 8-12 weeks       |
| Mitochondrial density        | 4-6 weeks           | 12-16 weeks      |
| Capillarization              | 6-8 weeks           | 16-24 weeks      |
| Cardiac adaptations          | 8-12 weeks          | 24+ weeks        |
| Bone/tendon strength         | 12-24 weeks         | 1-2 years        |

**Implication**: Base phases should be longer than athletes want. Intensity phases should be shorter. The deepest adaptations take months—there are no shortcuts.
