# Fuelling & Hydration

How to fuel training and daily life. Race-day fuelling is in `race-day.md`. The athlete's own eating plan, and any decision about counting or not counting, live in `Profile` and `Decisions`. These principles explain the plan and answer questions; they never override it.

## Daily Eating

Source: Galpin, Perform Q&A on nutrition (his summary of mainstream sports nutrition plus coaching opinion), unless noted.

- **Energy balance, not weight loss.** Under-fuelling costs performance and recovery first, then hormones and bone (low energy availability). Raise power, not power-to-weight by cutting weight: athletes who cut weight often lose absolute, and in Bu's tests even relative, VO2max (Bu; coach's experience). Training volume only pays if food, sleep and recovery keep up (Bu).
- **Carbohydrate in hard, high-volume training:** don't drop below about 5 g/kg/day (Galpin; coach's experience). When an athlete feels flat or low on energy, more carbohydrate is the first lever.
- **Protein:** extra muscle gain levels off at about 1.6 g/kg/day when energy is enough (Morton 2018 meta-analysis; research). Floor about 1.2 g/kg; endurance guidance 1.2–2.0 g/kg (ACSM 2016; research). Larger single doses are used, not wasted. On hard days protein must not crowd out carbohydrate (Claude's judgement).
- **Timing:** for muscle, protein timing matters little if the 24-hour total is enough (research). Carbohydrate soon after the first session matters when two sessions are less than about 8 hours apart (ACSM 2016; research).
- **Food quality:** mostly single-ingredient food, a variety of colours in fruit and vegetables, raw and cooked both. Fibre about 13–15 g per 1,000 kcal. Keep the meal before a quality or long session, and race-week meals, lower in fibre (Claude's judgement, standard sports-nutrition practice).
- **Liquid calories** are the hidden part of most diets, and the easiest way to add energy when heat or volume kills appetite: chocolate milk, juice, sports drink.
- **Change by adding first:** fill the gaps (carbohydrate, fluid, salt, a missing food group) before removing anything; supplements last.
- **Supplements:** none beyond food unless a test shows a deficiency, such as iron or vitamin D, tested and prescribed by a doctor (Bu; coach's experience).
- **Food safety in heat:** cooked food carried for hours before or after long sessions upsets the gut.

## Around Sessions

| Session                  | Before               | During                                         | After                                          |
| ------------------------ | -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Easy, under about 75 min | Normal meals         | Water                                          | Normal meal                                    |
| Quality                  | 25–50 g carbohydrate | Water; carbohydrate if over about 75 min       | Carbohydrate and protein at the next meal      |
| Long, over about 90 min  | A carbohydrate meal  | Carbohydrate per the gut-training step; sodium | Refill fluid (below), carbohydrate and protein |

Most sessions of 30–45 minutes need no carbohydrate during (Galpin). The table is Claude's judgement from the race-day ranges and ACSM 2016.

## Gut Training

The gut is trainable (research). Dose it and progress slowly.

- Start long sessions at about 30–50 g/h and step up by about 10 g/h every 2 weeks, toward the race target in `race-day.md`. Hold a step on bloating, cramps or nausea (Claude's judgement).
- Use the race products, or ones like them, in the later steps.
- Elite long-course athletes take 80–120 g/h. That's an elite number, not a default target (Claude's judgement).
- Long run: start with one carbohydrate dose about 40 minutes in, and add a second around 65 minutes once runs pass about 90 minutes (Claude's judgement).
- Write the fuel into the `Session` text of each long session, so the athlete sees it in the plan.

## Hydration

- **Daily baseline:** about half body weight in pounds, in ounces, before sweat losses (about 2.4 L for 75 kg). Enough for pale-yellow urine; not so much that the athlete pees hourly or wakes at night to go (Galpin; coach's experience).
- **During:** a measured sweat rate beats any formula. Drink 60–80% of measured losses in sessions over 90 minutes, with 500–1,000 mg sodium per litre (Claude's judgement within ACSM ranges). Galpin's rough rule of 90–180 ml every 15–20 minutes (about 270–700 ml/h) is a sanity check. In heat, measured needs can sit well above it. Gaining weight over a session means drinking too much.
- **Electrolytes:** water alone isn't enough in long or hot sessions. Sodium first.
- **Refill after:** about 1.25–1.5 L per kg of body weight lost, over the 2–4 hours after, with salty food or an electrolyte drink (ACSM 2016; research). Left alone, people drink back only about half.

### Sweat-Rate Test

1. Empty the bladder. Weigh nude or in dry minimal clothing before a long session in typical conditions.
2. Note everything drunk during the session.
3. Towel dry and weigh again the same way straight after.
4. Sweat loss (L) = weight before − weight after (kg) + fluid drunk (L). Sweat rate = loss ÷ hours.
5. The refill amount uses the weight change alone, because what was drunk is already counted in it.

Record it in `Tests` (Sport: Other, Protocol: sweat-rate test, Conditions: temperature, humidity, indoor or outdoor). Repeat in a hotter or cooler block. Sweat rate changes with heat and fitness.

## Caffeine

- Caffeine is an acute fix: it lifts today's session but doesn't repair accumulated fatigue. Sleep, reduced training, food and stress reduction are the chronic fixes (Galpin; coach's experience).
- A large dose before one session can cost the next one (Edgley, multi-day events; coach's experience, n = 1). Practise race-day caffeine in training (`race-day.md`).
